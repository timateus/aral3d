# Philosophy — Comparative Water-logy

> "For what Earth do we design?" — Benjamin Bratton, Kim Stanley Robinson, Lukas Likavčan.
> Echoing them, this project asks: **For what water do we design?**

This document is the project's *why*. The other docs describe what was built; this one describes the argument the build is making.

## The starting question

This project begins in the same place where Aral School begins: with the question **what water is**. Not where water is, not how much there is, not how it flows and evaporates — but what water is **imagined to be**, because what we imagine water to be defines how we engage with it.

From that question, a method: study various mental models of water — water as molecule, resource, spirit, infrastructure, play — in the eyes of rural kids, artists, politicians, cleaners, bazar workers, farmers. We call this a **comparative water-logy**: how water is expressed, experienced, visualized; what its political consequences and ecological implications are.

## Two scales

| Axis | Endpoints |
|---|---|
| **Engagement** | personal gesture ↔ planetary system |
| **Mode** | serious ↔ playful |

A local spot for swimming in the river is *playful and personal*. A canal network is *serious and planetary*. The recipe for challenging map thinking is finding perspectives where this dichotomy is disrupted — where the map is simultaneously serious and playful, engaging with large and small scale at the same time.

## The map mode of water

The project is particularly interested in the *map mode*: rivers as lines, lakes as polygons, an ecological disaster as something to be observed from above. This mode is powerful and necessary, but it carries its own limitations and implications. It tends to represent an ecosystem as a clean external visual object, while an ecosystem is precisely **neither clean nor external**.

Rivers get their share of misrepresentation on maps. Rivers constantly change course; their banks are porous; they follow the slope of terrain in ways hard to represent. A line makes a river feel like a pipe — it starts here, it ends there. But rivers leak and absorb. Rivers are the mud and silt they carry, the mosquitos and fishermen, the children swimming and willows growing.

Our method is not to reject maps, models or totality but to **hack them, appropriate them, test them, stress them, bend them and explore their limits**.

## Theoretical lineage

- **Bratton** — anti-anti-totality. Do not abandon the big picture; ask for a **multiplicity of totalities**, an egalitarian combination of models in which geopolitics, geoinfrastructures, irrigation, dams, cotton fields, schoolyards, gestures and dreams can be held in the same frame without being flattened into one master explanation.
- **Morton** — challenge the **data dump**. Ecological awareness does not follow from more information, more graphs, more cubic kilometres, more layers, more correct maps.
- **Haraway** — **stay with the trouble** through play, situated making, partial perspectives and shared responsibility.
- **Freire** — children are not recipients of ecological education but **co-authors** of the questions, tools, levels, stories and scenarios.
- **Dunne & Raby** — speculation is used not to solve problems but to make **alternative systems, interfaces and futures thinkable**.

## The recipe

A dam is not only an engineering object, but a geopolitical interface and a game mechanic. A cotton field is not only a flower, but geotechnology and terraforming. A river is not only a blue line, but a relation between snow, labour, state planning, extraction, soil, salinity, memory, fish, children, canals and futures.

A child's gesture in front of a screen is small, but it can move a simulated sea. The sea is enormous, but in the installation it can be held, played, broken, restored, misunderstood, and reimagined.

The point is not to make ecological crisis entertaining in a simple sense, but to use **play as a way of making complexity graspable without pretending that it has become simple**.

## What this means for the build

Practically, the project takes the serious data and mapping instrument already developed for the Aral Sea and turns it toward its own limits, its embedded assumptions, its aesthetic defaults and its political imagination. It tries to expose this apparatus publicly and performatively — not through critique alone, but through interaction, play, speculation, co-development and design.

What we arrived at is a **data and mapping platform that is also a video game**: real data, playful interfaces, gesture control, multiplayer modes, kids' agency and dreams, and an attempt to *experience* the ecosystem rather than only read about it.

## Audience as method

The platform is for schools, museums, festivals, scientists, policymakers, teenagers and casual visitors — not by giving each audience a separate simplified version of the Aral Sea, but by allowing **different models of water to appear through different modes of interaction**.

It is at once a curriculum tool, a museum installation, a speculative policy interface, a science and design fair exhibit, and a public storytelling environment. It spreads from discussion to prototype, prototype to classroom, classroom to museum, museum to ministry, ministry back to children, and children back into the platform.

At the Aral School Festival this becomes a play zone with video game controllers, gesture control, televisions, projections and interactive stations where visitors can "play" Aral Sea data. The work is developed by us and by teenagers from Karakalpakstan, rural and urban, so that they are not only users of ecological education but **authors** of levels, interactions, stories and scenarios.

The public does not simply look at a map of the Aral Sea. They move through it, compete inside it, collaborate with it, flood it, dry it, plant it, reroute it, break it, restore it, and test **what kind of water each action assumes**.

The question is no longer only *what happened to the Aral Sea*, or *what should be done with it*, but **what models of water are we playing with when we imagine its past, present and future**.
